Most tools marketed as "ICP scoring" are running a firmographic filter and calling it intent. If a platform can't tell you which specific post or comment triggered a match, and why that interaction fits your ICP better than someone else's, it isn't scoring intent. It's sorting a database by job title and company size, then relabeling the output.

## What real ICP scoring looks like, and what's just filtering

Real ICP scoring explains a match at the level of a specific interaction: this person commented on a post about outbound automation, and their company profile matches your target segment. Firmographic filtering only tells you a title and headcount line up. It has no idea whether the person has shown any interest in the problem you solve.

| What you're looking at | Firmographic filtering | Real ICP-based intent scoring |
|---|---|---|
| Basis for the match | Job title, industry, company size | The specific post, comment, or interaction, checked against your ICP |
| Explains the "why" | No, just a filter match | Yes, a per-interaction match narrative |
| Distinguishes buyer from competitor or job-seeker | No | Usually, based on what they engaged with |
| Data source | Static company/contact database | Live LinkedIn engagement plus your ICP definition |

Apollo is a good example of where the line actually sits. It gives you a large, searchable contact database, dozens of filters, and saved searches that flag new matches automatically, that part is real automation, not manual list-building. What it doesn't do is score a specific LinkedIn interaction against your ICP. Its intent-data add-on, built on Bombora and LeadSift, flags account-level, topic-based buying intent, which is useful, but it's a company-level signal, not tied to an individual's engagement or to a persistent ICP definition. Ask any tool that claims ICP scoring whether it can point to the post that triggered a match. If the answer is "we look at your saved filters," you're looking at filtering, not scoring.

## Why the distinction costs more when you're a team of one

Getting this wrong is expensive precisely because you don't have anyone to absorb the extra work it creates. If you're the first marketing hire at a Series A SaaS company, you already know the problem isn't finding tools. It's that every tool assumes you have a team behind you. Apollo assumes you already know your ICP precisely enough to build filters around it, and that someone's available to keep running those searches and working the list it returns. A separate intent platform assumes someone is already maintaining an ICP doc that the tool can plug into. A content tool assumes your positioning is locked and someone just needs to execute against it. You have none of that. You have a blank Notion page, a mandate to build pipeline, and maybe six months before the board asks why marketing hasn't produced anything.

The real cost of getting the firmographic-vs-intent distinction wrong isn't the subscription fee for the wrong tool. It's the context tax. Every tool needs to be told who your ICP is, what your product does, and how you talk about it. You paste your positioning into a saved search. You re-explain it in a prompt for outreach copy. You rebuild it again in a Notion doc nobody else opens. Every switch between tools is a small re-explanation of your company, and when you're one person doing the job of three, that tax adds up to hours a week you don't have.

Intent-based prospecting tools that scan LinkedIn for people liking, commenting, or engaging with content adjacent to your category solve half of this. They surface people already paying attention instead of a cold list pulled from a firmographic filter. But engagement alone isn't qualification: someone commenting on a post about sales automation might be your exact buyer, or a competitor, or a job seeker, or a student writing a paper. Without ICP scoring layered on top of that engagement signal, you're just trading one kind of noise, cold and unqualified lists, for another, warm and unqualified lists. And tools that only do ICP scoring have the opposite problem. They're built to rank a static list, which is fine if you already have a list, and dead weight if your real challenge is finding people in the first place.

## What to ask before you buy either kind of tool

Ask any vendor claiming to solve this two things: what specific interaction triggered this person's score, and does the tool remember your ICP the next time you use it, or do you re-enter it every session.

The distinction matters because reply rates depend on it. Cold, unpersonalized outreach performs the way it always does: low reply rates, regardless of channel. Outreach to people already scored against your ICP based on active engagement with your problem space runs meaningfully higher, because you're not guessing at relevance. You're responding to a signal the person already gave you.

For a solo marketing hire, the strongest argument for one connected system isn't just buy fewer tools to save money. It's that a shared context layer changes what you're capable of executing. Define your ICP, your voice, and your positioning once, and every downstream function reads from that same definition instead of losing hours to re-explaining your company every time you open a new tab. Platforms like COR build the scoring directly into a persistent ICP definition, so the per-post match explanation and the outreach copy it generates both draw from the same context instead of a fresh export every time.

## What this looks like once you can tell real scoring from filtering

Say a marketer is three weeks into the job at a Series A company with no documented ICP and no outbound motion. The fastest path isn't researching the best cold email tool. It's defining the ICP once, in enough detail to reuse: firmographics, the specific pain the product solves, the language buyers use when they talk about that pain. From there, the job is finding people on LinkedIn already engaging with that exact pain, checking whether a tool can explain why each one matches rather than just that their title does, and generating outreach that reads like it understands the buyer's situation.

That workflow doesn't require five vendors, and it doesn't require trusting a vendor's "ICP scoring" label at face value. It requires asking the one question that separates real scoring from relabeled filtering: what triggered this match, specifically.

## Quick Answer

Most tools claiming to score LinkedIn leads against your ICP are running a firmographic filter, matching on job title, industry, and company size, and calling it intent. Real ICP scoring explains a match at the level of the specific post or comment a person engaged with, not just their job title. Before buying any prospecting or intent tool, ask it to show you the interaction that triggered a given match; if it can't, you're looking at a filter, not a scoring engine. For a solo marketing hire, the further question is whether that tool remembers your ICP definition across sessions, or makes you re-enter it every time you use it.
